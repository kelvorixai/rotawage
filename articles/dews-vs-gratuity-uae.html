DEWS vs. gratuity: how the DIFC savings scheme actually works

If your employer is based in the DIFC, your end-of-service payout probably isn't calculated the way most people assume. Here's the actual mechanism, and why it can pay out more or less than standard gratuity.

Most UAE end-of-service guides — including the gratuity calculator on this site — describe the standard formula under Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021: a fixed number of days' basic pay per year of service. That formula does not apply if your employer is registered in the DIFC (Dubai International Financial Centre). Since February 2020, DIFC employees have been covered by a completely different system called DEWS — DIFC Employee Workplace Savings — and the difference in mechanics is worth understanding before you assume your gratuity calculator estimate applies to you.

How DEWS actually works

Instead of a lump-sum formula applied at the end of your employment, DEWS is a defined-contribution savings plan. Your employer pays a percentage of your basic salary only (not allowances) into an individual investment account every month, for as long as you're employed:

  • Under 5 years of service: 5.83% of basic salary per month
  • 5 years of service or more: 8.33% of basic salary per month

That money is invested through DFSA-regulated fund providers rather than sitting as a bookkeeping liability on your employer's balance sheet. You can typically also make voluntary top-up contributions from your own salary if you want to build the balance faster.

The mechanical difference that actually matters

Under standard gratuity, what you get at the end is a fixed calculation — 21 or 30 days of basic pay per year, regardless of what the market did while you worked there. Under DEWS, what you get is whatever your account balance actually is: contributions plus (or minus) investment performance. That makes DEWS closer to a workplace pension than a severance payment, and it means the amount is no longer fully predictable in advance the way a gratuity estimate is.

This cuts both ways. Over a long tenure with reasonable market conditions, an invested account can end up worth meaningfully more than the fixed formula would have paid. Over a short tenure, or a period where markets are down right when you leave, it can also underperform what standard gratuity would have given you. There's no guaranteed floor tied to your salary the way the 21/30-day formula provides.

What you can actually control

Most DEWS plans offer a choice between a small number of investment options — typically a default, professionally designed diversified fund (often built by an external asset manager) alongside more conservative or Sharia-compliant alternatives. The default option is built to suit a "typical" saver, but it's rarely the only choice available, and picking the wrong risk level for your own timeline is a common way people end up disappointed with the outcome.

Voluntary top-ups, where offered, are usually the biggest lever you personally control — the employer contribution rate is fixed, but nothing stops you from adding more if you want the account to grow faster than the mandatory minimum would produce on its own.

Is this coming to the rest of the UAE?

DEWS itself is specific to DIFC employers, but similar defined-contribution alternatives to standard gratuity have been expanding. The ADGM (Abu Dhabi Global Market) introduced its own optional end-of-service savings framework effective April 2025, and a voluntary alternative savings scheme has been available to mainland UAE employers since late 2023 through MOHRE-approved providers, with the government continuing to refine the framework. If you change employers, it's worth checking early which system — if any — covers your new role, rather than assuming standard gratuity applies everywhere.
