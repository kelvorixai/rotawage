How big should your emergency fund be on a rotational income?

"Three to six months of expenses" is the standard advice — but it's built around a monthly paycheck that never stops. Here's what actually changes the number for a rotational or FIFO income.

The standard emergency-fund rule — save three to six months of living expenses — comes from a specific assumption: that your income is a fixed amount, arriving reliably every month, that could stop entirely if you lost your job. That's a reasonable baseline for a conventional salary. It's not quite the right frame for a rotational, offshore, or FIFO contract, where the risk profile is different in ways worth pricing in separately, not just applying the same rule by default.

What's actually different about the risk

A rotational contract carries two risks a flat-salary emergency fund isn't really built for. First, income concentration: your pay often comes from a single employer and sometimes a single site or project, so a contract non-renewal, project wind-down, or company-level downsizing can end the income source more abruptly than a typical office job's notice-period structure. Second, the cycle itself creates timing risk that has nothing to do with losing your job — an emergency that happens to land during a home stretch, right after a cycle's earnings have already been spent or routed elsewhere, behaves differently than one that lands the week before payday for someone on a flat monthly income.

A more useful way to size it

Rather than picking a number of months and stopping there, it's worth separating the fund into what it's actually protecting against:

  • Contract-loss coverage: how long would it realistically take to find comparable work if this contract ended without warning? For in-demand technical or specialist roles this might be shorter than a generalist role; for a narrow or saturated specialty, it can run considerably longer. This part of the fund should be sized against realistic job-search time in your specific field, not a generic three-month assumption.
  • Mid-cycle timing buffer: a smaller, separate amount sized to cover an unplanned expense that lands during a low-cash point in the cycle — closer to one cycle's worth of ordinary expenses than a multi-month figure, since its job is to smooth timing, not replace lost income.

Adding these two together usually produces a number different from — and for many rotational contracts, larger than — the generic "three to six months" figure, because it's accounting for a real risk (concentrated income source) that a diversified-employer, steady-paycheck household doesn't carry in the same way.

Where to actually keep it

The instinct on a rotational income is sometimes to let the on-site cycle's surplus simply accumulate in a current account as an informal buffer. That works until it doesn't — an account with no clear purpose is also the easiest account to quietly spend down during a home stretch. Treating the emergency fund as a separate, named account (even a second savings account at the same bank) makes it something to protect rather than something that happens to have a balance.

Liquidity matters more here than the last basis point of return. A locked-in product with an attractive rate — the kind of thing covered in the sukuk guide on this site — is specifically the wrong home for this money, precisely because the whole point of an emergency fund is being able to reach it without a penalty or a wait, on a timeline you don't get to choose in advance.

The actual takeaway

The generic rule isn't wrong, it's just calibrated for a different income shape. Building the number from the two real risks — how long a job search might actually take in your field, and how much cushion a mid-cycle emergency needs — usually produces a more honest target than importing a rule built for someone else's income pattern.
